In-Custody Informant



View the statement of an in-custody informant against the defendant with caution and close scrutiny. In evaluating such testimony, you should consider the extent to which it may have been influenced by the receipt of, or expectation of, any benefits. This does not mean that you may arbitrarily disregard such statement, but you should give it the weight to which you find it to be entitled in the light of all the evidence in the case.

If you decide that a declarant was an in-custody informant, then you may not convict the defendant of example charged crime s based on the statement of that in-custody informant alone.

You may use the statement of an in-custody informant against the defendant only if:

1. The statement is supported by other evidence that you believe;

2. That supporting evidence is independent of the statement;

AND

3. That supporting evidence connects the defendant to the commission of the crimes. The supporting evidence is not sufficient if it merely shows that the charged crime was committed.

This supporting evidence requirement does not apply where the testimony of an in-custody informant is offered for any purpose other than proving guilt or evidence in aggravation.

Supporting evidence, however, may be slight. It does not need to be enough, by itself, to prove that the defendant is guilty of the charged crime, and it does not need to support every fact about which the witness testified. On the other hand, it is not enough if the supporting evidence merely shows that a crime was committed or the circumstances of its commission. The supporting evidence must tend to connect the defendant to the commission of the crime.

A percipient witness is someone who personally perceived the matter that he or she testified about.
